# Working from reference material

The user hands you screenshots, links, a brand book, or "make it feel like
X". References are direction, not duplication: the goal is to understand
*why* the reference works and rebuild that force with your own content.
Copying produces a worse version of someone else's site; transposing
produces yours.

## The extraction protocol

Run this inventory over each reference (actually look — open links in the
browser pane, study screenshots at full size):

1. **Type:** what families/classes (grotesque? serif? mono?), how many, the
   display-to-body size ratio, weight range, tracking habits, case habits
   (all-caps labels? sentence-case headings?).
2. **Color:** dominant field, surface steps, accent(s) and *how often* the
   accent appears, warm or cool neutrals, dark/light bias.
3. **Space:** dense or airy, section rhythm, container width, how much the
   layout breathes around its hero.
4. **Layout:** grid columns, symmetry vs deliberate breaks, how images are
   framed (full-bleed? bordered? tilted?), nav pattern.
5. **Motion:** speeds, what animates (everything? one thing?), scroll
   behavior, hover personality.
6. **Texture & depth:** borders vs shadows, grain/noise, illustration or
   photo style, corner radii.
7. **Voice:** how the copy sounds — terse and technical? warm? conspiratorial?
   Copy is part of the design language.

## Find "the move"

Every strong reference has **one decision doing most of the work** — the
oversized lowercase wordmark, the hairline-everything panel system, the
acid-on-cream palette, the way every image is a tilted polaroid. Name it in
one sentence. If you take only one thing from a reference, take the move —
transposed through your own content — and you'll capture more of its energy
than by copying ten surface details.

## Build a style tile before building the site

Translate the inventory into **your** tokens — palette swatches, type
specimen (one display setting + one body block), spacing base, radius/shadow
choice, one sample component — and show it or describe it to the user for a
cheap yes/no *before* investing in full pages. Corrections at the tile stage
cost minutes; at the built-site stage they cost the afternoon.

## Multiple references

Pick **one dominant** reference for structure and feel; harvest at most an
accent each from the others (a color idea from B, a hover behavior from C).
Averaging three references produces mush — design languages don't blend,
they collide.

## Transpose, never trace

- Rebuild the *system* (ratios, rhythm, restraint), not the artifacts. Your
  hero copy, your imagery, your palette shifted to the project's brand.
- **Never reproduce** another company's logo, illustrations, photography,
  mascot, or distinctive trade dress on a lookalike product — that's not
  inspiration, it's a legal and ethical problem. Same for pay-walled
  fonts: substitute a same-class face (a grotesque for a grotesque) rather
  than pirating the exact one.
- If the user asks for a pixel clone of a real site, flag the line: match
  the *quality bar and feel*, keep the identity distinct.

## When the reference is bad

Sometimes the reference has problems (unreadable contrast, chaotic type).
Extract what the user *loves about it* — ask if unclear — deliver that force
with the craft problems quietly fixed, and note what you changed. "Like the
reference, but it passes contrast" is a deliverable; silently importing its
defects is not.
